## Monsters

Great heroes need horrendous antagonists. This chapter is about how to create and play as those antagonists — from the lowly goblin warrior to the hellish demon.

### Using Monsters

A monster is any living (or undead) thing that stands in the characters' way. How you use monsters follows directly from your agenda and principles. Your first agenda is to *portray a fantastic world* — describing monsters and adversaries in vivid detail brings them to life.

The player characters are the heroes. Monsters exist to illustrate what a dangerous, awful place Dungeon World can be; you shouldn't be rooting for them to win, but they may challenge, and even sometimes defeat, the heroes. The principle of *think dangerous* sums this up: think of every monster like an arrow fired at the characters, ammunition for the danger you're presenting. A monster stops being mere ammunition when something in the world puts the spotlight on it — a spout lore roll, the players' answers building interest, a battle that leaves a lasting impression. When that happens, give the monster a name and consider creating a danger to represent it.

One thing your agenda and principles don't say anything about is setting up a fair fight. Your first responsibility is to the fiction and to giving the characters a real threat, not to a balanced encounter. Dungeon World isn't about balancing encounter levels; it's about adventure and death-defying feats.

### Elements of a Monster

Every monster has **moves** that describe its behavior and abilities — just like GM moves, they can be hard or soft depending on the move and circumstances. Each monster has an **instinct** describing its goals at a high level, the guide for how to use it in the fiction. The monster's **description** is where all its other features come from.

**Damage** is a die to roll, maybe with modifiers, dealt whenever the monster causes physical harm. Monsters have **tags** describing how they deal damage, including range — out of range, no damage — plus any tags that could go on a weapon (messy, slow, etc.). Special monster-only tags describe key attributes like size and organization.

A monster's **HP** measures how much damage it can take before it dies; at 0 HP it's dead, no last breath. Some monsters have **armor**, working just like player armor. **Special qualities** describe innate aspects important to play — a quality like intangible means mundane stuff just passes through it, so a mundane sword swing isn't Hack and Slash against it.

**Monsters Without Stats.** Some creatures operate on a scale so far beyond the mortal that HP, armor, and damage don't apply, or they just aren't dangerous in a fight. Don't assign them stats — they can still have tags, and still act according to instinct and moves.

### Monster Tags

*Magical:* Magical through and through. *Devious:* Its main danger lies beyond the simple clash of battle. *Amorphous:* Bizarre, unnatural anatomy. *Organized:* Has a group structure that aids survival — defeating one may draw the wrath of others, or sound an alarm. *Intelligent:* Smart enough that individuals pick up other skills. *Hoarder:* Almost certainly has treasure. *Stealthy:* Avoids detection, prefers surprise. *Terrifying:* Presence and appearance evoke fear. *Cautious:* Prizes survival over aggression. *Construct:* Made, not born. *Planar:* From beyond this world.

**Organization Tags:** *Horde* (where there's one, there's a lot more) · *Group* (usually 3–6 or so) · *Solitary* (lives and fights alone).

**Size Tags:** *Tiny* (much smaller than a halfling) · *Small* (about halfling size) · *Large* (bigger than a human, about cart-sized) · *Huge* (as big as a small house or larger).

### Making Monsters

Monsters start with your description — what it looks like, what it does, why it stands out, the stories told about it. When you need stats, work through this series of questions, noting the tags and stats each answer grants without answering aloud. If a combination would reduce HP or damage below 1, it stays at 1. If your finished monster has only one move and you'll use it often, give it 1–2 more of your own devising.

**What is it known to do?** Write a monster move describing what it does.

**What does it want that causes problems for others?** This is its instinct — write it as an intended action.

**How does it usually hunt or fight?** In large groups: horde, d6 damage, 3 HP. In small groups, 2–5: group, d8 damage, 6 HP. All by its lonesome: solitary, d10 damage, 12 HP.

**How big is it?** Smaller than a house cat: tiny, hand, -2 damage. Halfling-esque: small, close. About human size: close. As big as a cart: large, close, reach, +4 HP, +1 damage. Much larger than a cart: huge, reach, +8 HP, +3 damage.

**What is its most important defense?** Cloth or flesh: 0 armor. Leathers or thick hide: 1 armor. Mail or scales: 2 armor. Plate or bone: 3 armor. Permanent magical protection: 4 armor, magical.

**What is it known for?** (Choose all that apply.) Unrelenting strength: +2 damage, forceful. Skill in offense: roll damage twice, take the better roll. Skill in defense: +1 armor. Deft strikes: +1 piercing. Uncanny endurance: +4 HP. Deceit and trickery: stealthy, write a move about dirty tricks. A useful adaptation (amphibious, wings): add a special quality. The favor of the gods: divine, +2 damage or +2 HP or both. Spells and magic: magical, write a move about its spells.

**What is its most common form of attack?** Note it with the creature's damage, then answer: vicious and obvious armaments: +2 damage. Keeps others at bay: reach. Small and weak armaments: reduce damage die size by one. Can slice or pierce metal: messy, +1 piercing (or +3 if it can tear metal apart). Armor doesn't help against it: ignores armor. Attacks at range: near or far or both.

**Which of these describe it?** (Choose all that apply.) Not dangerous for its wounds, but for other reasons: devious, reduce damage die size by one, write a move about why it's dangerous. Organizes for support: organized, write a move about calling for help. As smart as a human: intelligent. Actively defends with a shield or similar: cautious, +1 armor. Collects valuables: hoarder. From beyond this world: planar, write a move about its otherworldly knowledge. Kept alive beyond simple biology: +4 HP. Was made by someone: construct, add a quality or two about its construction. Disturbing or horrible appearance: terrifying, write a quality about why. No organs or discernible anatomy: amorphous, +1 armor, +3 HP. Ancient — older than man, elves, and dwarves: increase damage die size by one. Abhors violence: roll damage twice, take the worst result.

### Treasure

When players search a monster's belongings, describe them honestly. If it's accumulated wealth, roll that randomly, starting with its damage die, modified if the monster is: Hoarder (roll damage die twice, take higher), Far from home (add at least one ration), Magical (some strange item, possibly magical), Divine (a sign of a deity), Planar (something not of this earth), Lord over others (+1d4 to the roll), Ancient and noteworthy (+1d4 to the roll). Then roll the damage die (plus any added dice) against this table:

1. A few coins, 2d8 or so
2. An item useful to the current situation
3. Several coins, about 4d10
4. A small item (gem, art) worth as much as 2d10×10 coins, 0 weight
5. Some minor magical trinket
6. Useful information (clues, notes, etc.)
7. A bag of coins, 1d4×100 or so, 1 weight per 100
8. A very valuable small item (gem, art) worth 2d6×100, 0 weight
9. A chest of coins and valuables, 1 weight, worth 3d6×100 coins
10. A magical item or magical effect
11. Many bags of coins totaling 2d4×100 or so
12. A sign of office (crown, banner) worth at least 3d4×100 coins
13. A large art item worth 4d4×100 coins, 1 weight
14. A unique item worth at least 5d4×100 coins
15. Everything needed to learn a new spell, and roll again
16. A portal or secret path (or directions to one), and roll again
17. Something relating to one of the characters, and roll again
18. A hoard: 1d10×1000 coins and 1d10×10 gems worth 2d6×100 each

### Monster Settings

The monsters in this book are presented in *monster settings* — a way of grouping monsters by the type of location where they're found, rather than by species or origin. A monster setting tells you what kind of monsters might inhabit an area, while your fronts tell you what monsters are working together or have ongoing plots. Consult a monster setting to populate a front or when you want a threat only tangentially related to one of your fronts.

The seven monster settings covered in this book:

- **Cavern Dwellers** — goblins, ankhegs, gargoyles, purple worms, and other denizens of the caves and tunnels below the mountains.
- **The Lower Depths** — aboleths, dragons, minotaurs, nagas, and the deep-dwelling terrors of ancient ruins and the world's heart.
- **Twisted Experiments** — chimeras, golems, manticores, owlbears, and other creations of arcane hubris.
- **Folk of the Realm** — the ordinary and not-so-ordinary people of the world: bandits, knights, nobles, spies, and more.
- **Ravenous Hordes** — formians, gnolls, orcs, and tritons, organized for war and conquest.
- **Planar Powers** — angels, devils, djinn, and the other creatures that slip through from beyond this world.
- **Swamp Denizens** — basilisks, hydras, lizardmen, trolls, and the rot and ruin of the fens.
- **Undead Legions** — ghosts, liches, vampires, zombies, and the many shapes death's magic takes when it doesn't take.
- **The Dark Woods** — centaurs, dryads, treants, werewolves, and the sylvan monsters of the ancient forest.
