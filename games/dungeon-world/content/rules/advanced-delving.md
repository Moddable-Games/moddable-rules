## Advanced Delving

Dungeon World portrays a specific kind of fantasy adventure — one with elves and dwarves, heroes and villains, and characters struggling for riches and glory in a dangerous world. Maybe you've got an idea for something different. All that is possible (and, in fact, encouraged) with a little effort. This chapter explains how you can turn this Dungeon World into *your* Dungeon World.

### Making Moves

The best place to start hacking Dungeon World is with the moves. You might want to create moves to reflect the effects of some particular threat, to cover something particularly important to your setting, or, as you get more experienced, to expand a class or create your own class entirely.

**Getting Started.** You can start a move with the trigger — some action that just feels like it should be a move. You can start with the effect, particularly useful for class moves: you know casting a spell is something the wizard does, so what triggers it? Rarely, you can start with the mechanics, but be wary of any idea that's entirely mechanical, since moves always start and end with the fiction. You can always use a move from another PbtA game too, modified for Dungeon World.

**Types of Moves.** What role a move fulfills determines what kind of move you're creating. Moves for the environment or special features are special moves, usually the GM's domain. Moves reflecting a special competency tied to a specific class are class moves; if tied to a concept multiple classes might access, consider a compendium class. A move the players do but not tied to a specific class or theme is a basic or special move (basic if it comes up often, special if rarely). Moves players make in response to monsters are rare player moves; moves monsters make against players aren't player moves at all — they're monster moves, simple statements of what the monster does.

**World Moves.** Your world is full of fantastic things that might deserve custom moves reflecting exactly what they do — tied strongly to a particular place at a particular time, calling out something as important rather than just relying on Defy Danger.

**Class Moves.** When adding a move to a class, look carefully at what class it belongs to. Avoid giving a class moves that infringe on another class's expertise. Be careful with moves that duplicate an existing benefit even under a different trigger — damage and armor increases in particular should be handled carefully, since the classes' current numbers reflect the overall danger level of the game.

### New Classes

Once you've created moves and customized existing classes, you'll notice a class is just a collection of themed moves that work together. Creating a new class is the next natural step.

Think first about how the class relates to existing ones — no character exists in isolation. An excellent first step is to think about fictional characters you'd like to take inspiration from, using them as a guide for what's cool rather than slavishly recreating their abilities.

With a clear idea in mind, work through: **HP** (base+Constitution; base is almost always 4, 6, 8, or 10 — 4 for deliberately fragile classes, 6 for those not ready to fight but able to take a hit, 8 for those who can mix it up a little, 10 for skilled warriors), **damage** (a single die from d4–d12, though other options like 2d4 or 1d6+2 are possible; high HP and damage tend to go together, but a class could be a pacifistic wall or a fragile glass cannon), **alignment** (most classes should have Neutral as an option; a good alignment move happens with some regularity and guides toward a particular type of action, showing something true about the character), **bonds** (unless the class is particularly social or antisocial, write four, adding or removing based on how connected the class is; avoid bonds dictating a moral stance, and avoid proper names in starting bonds), **look** (an excellent spot to reflect your fictional inspiration; include at least one clothing choice), and **equipment** (always include at least one weapon and one armor option unless the class clearly lacks fighting skill, plus dungeon rations).

**Compendium Classes.** A compendium class is only available to higher-level characters who meet specific requirements — a way to layer a concept onto multiple other classes. The basic structure is a starting move available only after a specific experience (not a stat prerequisite), taken instead of a normal class move at the next level gained, plus 2–3 further moves that require the starting move. Compendium classes suit concepts that don't quite inspire a full class or that overlap with existing classes.

### Adventure Moves

Adventure moves deal directly with the adventure underway — they can move the action along, change rewards, or transition between adventures. They're especially useful for short games (a convention slot, a con game) where you want to front-load "the adventure so far" and jump straight into play in media res, or to adapt the End of Session move's questions to tie XP directly to the specific adventure you're running (make sure to show players the new questions — the goal is to make XP awards tie to the adventure, not to keep them in the dark).

**Move Structure.** Moves always follow the same structure: a trigger ("when…") and an effect ("then…"). Triggers are fictional actions, though they can also fire at character creation or the start/end of a session; a trigger never deals with precise units of time — Dungeon World's pacing is fluid, like a movie, not bound by rounds or minutes.

Broad trigger types: when a character takes action (Discern Realities); when a character takes action under specific circumstances (Hack and Slash); when circumstances dictate, with no character action (End of Session); when a character uses a thing (a magic item); from now on (an ongoing condition).

Broad effect types: roll; substitute stats; negate damage; give a bonus or penalty, forward or ongoing; deal or heal damage; choose options; hold and spend; ask and answer; change circumstances; mark experience; call for more information; add options.

### Changing the Basics

Moves can change the basic structure of the game itself, but be very careful with moves that muck with the fundamentals — moves should never contradict the GM's principles or agenda, or break the basic "take the action to gain the effect" rule. Some parts of the game are easy to change, like the XP required to level or what actions are rewarded with it (just make sure to share any change with your players before you start). If you want to make something harder — fighting a dragon, say — the best answer is usually that it's harder because it's fictionally stronger, not because of an added mechanical penalty layered onto every roll.

### Development of a Move

Hack and Slash was one of the earliest Dungeon World moves, and it went through several major revisions before reaching its current form. Early versions offered choices that didn't all flow naturally from a single fictional attack (dividing damage among multiple targets from one swing, for instance) or reduced the effects of an attack to nothing but HP loss, when monsters do so much more than just take away hit points. Each revision scoped the move down to only the effects that could clearly follow from a single melee attack, and reworded the options so a monster could "attack" — not just deal damage — opening up interesting monster moves in response. This is the general shape of good move design: keep tightening the fictional link between trigger and effect until nothing in the move could happen any other way.

### The GM

Writing new GM moves is easy, since a GM move is just a statement of something that fictionally happens — feel free to write new ones as you please, keeping the spectrum of hard to soft moves, your principles, and your agenda in mind.

Changing the GM's agenda or principles is a bigger change, likely requiring changes throughout the rest of the game plus playtesting. **Play to find out what happens** is the least changeable part of the agenda — the moves give players abilities that can change a planned adventure quickly, so departing from this agenda means resisting the moves at every step. **Fill the characters' lives with adventure** is hard to change too, since the move structure and the effects of a miss are built around it. **Portraying a fantastic world** is the easiest part of the agenda to change, though it still requires rethinking many moves — a historical, grim, or utopian world are all possible with enough rewriting.

Of the principles, **Address the characters, not the players; Make your move, but misdirect; Never speak the name of your move; Begin and end with the fiction;** and **Be a fan of the characters** are most important — without them the conversation of play and the use of moves is likely to break down. **Embrace the fantastic; Give every monster life; Name every person; Think Dangerous;** and **Give them something to work towards** are key to Dungeon World's spirit specifically, and changing them amounts to changing the setting. **Leave Blanks; Sometimes, let them decide;** and **Ask questions and use the answers** are important to running Dungeon World well, but portable to many other games in a similar style.

### Monsters

The easiest place to modify monsters is in the questions used to create them — adjusting lethality or randomness to your liking, or changing the questions to present a different view of monsters. The default questions imply monsters are more or less like other creatures, of many alignments, not always opposed to the players. If you want Dungeon World to be about hunting down evil monsters and destroying them, you might rewrite the questions to reflect that instead. When creating new monster questions, either reinterpret existing monsters by answering them again, or only use the new questions for new monsters going forward.
