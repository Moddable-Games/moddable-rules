## Atlantic City

The Atlantic City variant is the community folk version of The Landlord's Game that circulated among a group of Quakers in Atlantic City, New Jersey from 1929 onwards. It uses the rules of the 1906 Commercial Edition with the property names replaced by the names of Atlantic City streets — chosen because those were the streets where members of the community lived.

### History

Ruth Hoskins, a teacher at the Atlantic City Friends School, brought a hand-copied version of the game from Indianapolis in 1929. She had learned it from Pete and James Daggett, friends of Dan Layman who had developed an earlier version. Hoskins introduced the game to Cyril and Ruth Harvey, fellow Quakers and teachers, who began hosting regular game nights and made their own copy of the board using street names personal to their community.

Jesse Raiford, an Atlantic City real estate agent and friend of the Harveys, contributed two significant additions: he grouped the properties by colour and assigned prices to each, replacing the auction mechanic with fixed purchase values. The colours and values he chose reflected the real geography of Atlantic City at the time, with the cheapest properties in the low-income districts near Baltic and Mediterranean Avenues and the highest-value properties on the seafront at Park Place and the Boardwalk.

The board Hoskins created named the cheapest property "Arctic Avenue." Charles Darrow, who encountered the Harvey version of the game at a dinner party in 1933, later changed this name to "Mediterranean Avenue" when he produced his own copy. Darrow subsequently sold the game to Parker Brothers in 1935 under the name Monopoly, representing it as his own invention. In court testimony documented during the Anspach litigation of the 1970s, Ruth Hoskins described the property selection process street by street, and confirmed that Connecticut, Vermont, and Oriental Avenues were names she herself proposed.

The Atlantic City folk version is therefore the direct ancestor of the world's best-selling commercial board game.

### Properties

The Atlantic City version uses the 40-space board layout of the 1906 Commercial Edition. The property spaces are named after streets in and around Atlantic City, New Jersey, grouped by colour and increasing in value around the board. The street names and their groupings are documented in court testimony (Anspach v. Parker Brothers, 1970s) and in Mary Pilon's historical account (The Monopolists, 2015).

The street names are real Atlantic City locations. Several no longer exist as originally named: Illinois Avenue was renamed Martin Luther King Jr. Boulevard in the 1980s, St. Charles Place was redeveloped with the Showboat Casino, and "Marven Gardens" was a misspelling of Margate City's "Marven Gardens" introduced when Hoskins's friends copied the board by hand.

### Setup

As per the 1906 Commercial Edition. Property names on Title Deed cards use Atlantic City street names. All other components are unchanged.

### Rules

As per the 1906 Commercial Edition. All rules apply unchanged. The sole difference from the 1906 edition is the property names and their colour groupings and prices as established by Jesse Raiford.

### Winning

As per the 1906 Commercial Edition: most wealth at end of game, counting Title Deed cards at face value, plus houses and hotels at cost, plus cash on hand.
